# Singing Bowl Acoustics and Physical Modeling

A singing bowl (a kind of standing bell) supports many long-lived, slightly inharmonic modes.  Typically made of a bronze-like alloy (copper, tin, etc.), the bowl’s tone depends on its size, thickness, and material composition.  Playing technique – a strike or sustained rubbing – excites **two families of modes** (radial and tangential) at near-similar frequencies.  Because actual bowls are not perfectly symmetric, their modes beat against each other, producing the characteristic shimmering sound.  Past research confirms that real bowls have rich **partial spectra** and strong beating: for example Wilkerson et al. analyzed a bowl’s impulse response to extract main resonance frequencies and bandwidths.  Inacio et al. report that alloy composition and shape shift both mode frequencies and damping, and they model the bowl as an **axisymmetric shell** with multiple modal resonances excited by normal impact or friction.  In summary, a faithful model must capture *several resonant modes with adjustable frequencies and damping* and allow two excitation styles (impact vs. continuous rubbing).

# Resonator and Modal Synthesis Techniques

Several efficient approaches exist for modeling resonant objects in real time.  A **modal synthesis** (resonator bank) approach uses a small set of damped resonant filters tuned to the bowl’s partials.  Each mode can be implemented as a second-order resonant filter (e.g. a band-pass or state-variable filter) whose center frequency and decay time match a measured mode.  An impulse (strike) feeds all modes at once (with gains weighted by striking position).  A sustained rubbing can be modeled by a nonlinear **friction driver** that continuously injects energy into the modes (like a bowed string model). 

Another approach is **banded waveguide synthesis**, introduced by Essl & Cook.  Banded waveguides split the instrument’s spectrum into frequency bands, each realized as a tuned digital waveguide delay line.  This method captures dispersion and inharmonicity by aligning each band with an actual resonance of the bowl.  Wikipedia notes that “banded waveguide synthesis is used to simulate sounds of dispersive objects” including singing bowls and bells.  In practice, one would create ~5–10 waveguide resonators, each tuned to a harmonic or inharmonic partial.  Because banded waveguides are effectively modal filters, they also allow nonlinear excitation and parameter changes in real time.

In summary, **modal/banded-synthesis** is most practical for VCV Rack.  It lets us select the key partials explicitly and adjust them cheaply.  For example, Wilkerson et al. modeled each partial with a pair of digital waveguides (simulating waves on both sides of the bowl) to reproduce beatings.  A simpler implementation could use direct-form biquad filters or state-variable resonators (which are already available in many DSP libraries) to emulate each mode.  This avoids the heavy math of full finite-element shells, keeping CPU load low.

# Material Properties: Metal vs. Crystal

To let users switch between “metal bowl” and “crystal bowl,” we note the key material differences.  A typical metal (bronze/brass) bowl has **high density (~8.8–9.0 g/cm³)** and high stiffness (Young’s modulus ~110–120 GPa).  In contrast, a fused quartz (crystal) bowl is much lighter (density ~2.20 g/cm³) and slightly less stiff (E ~71.7 GPa).  The sound speed ratio (sqrt(E/ρ)) is actually somewhat larger in quartz, meaning a same-size quartz bowl would resonate higher.  In practice, crystal bowls often sound purer and have longer sustain with less internal damping.  

We can capture this by parameterizing the **inharmonicity and damping**.  For example, the resonant frequencies $f_n$ of a circular shell go roughly like $f_n \propto \alpha_n \sqrt{E/\rho}$, where $\alpha_n$ depends on geometry and mode index.  A “material” knob could interpolate between a metal default and a quartz default for each partial.  It might slightly shift frequencies and adjust decay times (Q-factors).  Since crystal bowls are often made of glassy silica with lower internal friction, one could set higher Q (longer decay) in that mode; metal bowls (and soft mallets) introduce more loss, so modes decay faster.  As a rough guide, glassy silica has a very high Q in optics, so acoustically a crystal bowl might have Q of thousands, while a typical bronze bell mode might have Q in the hundreds.  Tuning these by ear (and possibly referencing measured envelopes) would yield the desired effect.

Mallets add more variability.  Hard mallets (metal or hardwood) excite many high-frequency overtones and introduce an impulsive, bright attack.  Softer mallets (rubber, felt) emphasize lower modes and damp the spectrum.  In modeling, we can simulate mallet hardness by varying the *excitation spectrum*: e.g. a very short impulse for a hard strike, or a slower ramp for a soft hit.  For the rubbing mode, a suede-covered mallet (common for singing bowls) produces continuous friction: one can model this by coupling a constant-velocity boundary condition with a Coulomb or Dahl friction model, which drives the resonators continuously.  Control parameters would include the *force* and *speed* of the rub, which modulate how strongly and steadily each mode is driven.

# Computational Cost and Real-Time Feasibility

Modern CPUs can handle quite a few second-order filters at audio rates.  Suppose we use 8–12 modes, each a biquad or state-variable filter updated per sample.  A typical IIR resonator requires on the order of 4–6 multiplies per sample.  Thus 12 modes might need <100 multiplies/adds per sample per voice – around a few million operations per second.  Even accounting for extra cost (friction equations, control logic, parameter updates), this is quite light on a desktop CPU.  Wilkerson et al. explicitly targeted **real-time performance**: they built a Pure Data extern (written in C) called *bowl~* that ran at audio rate.  They also used a simple *inverse filtering* of the impulse to get the attack transient, then left the resonators to handle decay, which is efficient.  In Rack, one could similarly preprocess an impulse to shape the hit, then feed it to the resonators.

If using banded waveguides, each band is essentially a tuned delay line.  Even an explicit delay-line per partial would add some delay memory cost, but the sample-by-sample CPU cost is similar to an IIR.  Another known trick is the use of **modal filter designs** (as in STK’s ModalSynth or SuperCollider’s ResonFM).  Many audio frameworks have modal resonator classes that take a list of frequency/damping pairs; using such a library routine could expedite development.  In VCV Rack’s C++ environment, one could implement each resonator with `dsp::IIR::Kopec` or `dsp::Biquad` classes, or use a hand-rolled direct-form if desired. 

A reasonable estimate: 10 resonators × (say) 10 ops/resonator = 100 ops per sample.  At 48 kHz, that’s 4.8 million ops/sec – trivial for a modern CPU.  Even adding a few nonlinear friction calculations or control I/O would leave plenty of headroom.  Profile-run some prototypes: for example, compute how many CPU cycles are spent per filter update, then scale by 48000.  But empirically, other Rack modules (e.g. complex delays or multiple filters) use similar budgets and run on a single audio thread easily. 

# Implementation and Parameter Mapping

For a VCV Rack module, the interface could include: **Bowl Size/Pitch**, **Material (Metal/Quartz)**, **Mallet Hardness/Type**, **Strike vs. Rub**, and **Excitation Intensity**.  

- **Size/Pitch**: Map a knob or CV to the fundamental pitch.  In the model, increasing size lowers all mode frequencies (roughly inversely).  One can precompute partial ratios for a reference bowl size, then scale them with pitch.  (E.g. an analytic formula: $f_{nm} = k_{nm} \sqrt{E/\rho}/R^2$ for a thin shell of radius $R$ – but tuning by ear or interpolation from measurements is fine.)  

- **Material**: A selector or CV between “Metal” and “Quartz” could adjust two sets of parameters: (a) the inharmonicity of higher modes (crystal bowls tend to be closer to harmonic); (b) the damping (quartz = less, metal = more); and optionally (c) timbral brightness (maybe increase high-frequency content for metal).  For instance, use a slightly higher ratio of partial frequencies or lower Q for metal.

- **Mallet Hardness**: A knob (Hard–Soft) could modulate the excitation envelope.  Hard = short, full-band impulse (and maybe a small metallic “click” added).  Soft = gentler envelope (longer attack, maybe low-pass filtered).  The striker’s material could also modulate the coupling coefficient: a very soft mallet might not excite the highest modes at all, effectively diminishing their gain.

- **Strike vs. Rub**: Likely two inputs/triggers or a mode switch.  In *strike mode*, any trigger (gate or envelope follower) would cause a one-shot excitation.  In *rub mode*, a continuous input (rotary encoder or CV) representing stick speed would feed a friction model.  The friction model would generate a (nonlinear) force signal that drives the resonators each sample, as in a bowed-string model.  One could even use an existing Rack library for friction or a simple Coulomb friction equation (`F = μ * v`, with clip to zero when sticking, etc.).  

- **Strike Location**: Optional, but playing point on a bowl changes which modes are most excited (striking near the rim emphasizes certain overtones).  We could approximate this by giving a second CV that skews the mode amplitudes (e.g. a bias between odd vs even modes).  If computational budget allows, a more accurate approach is to multiply each mode’s output by the mode shape value at the strike point.  But a simpler mapping (e.g. "Edge vs Center" knob) could suffice.

For parameter mapping references: Wilkerson et al. used rubboard controllers mapping position to force/velocity and to select bowl size/material.  Similarly, you might map CVs to physical analogs: e.g. a CV knob for material chooses one of two preset sets of resonator parameters, while another CV for size scales all frequencies linearly.  

# Summary

In building a VCV Rack singing-bowl module, one should combine **modal or banded-waveguide synthesis** with controllable excitation models.  The core idea is to simulate a handful of decaying resonant modes (reflecting the bowl’s partials) and drive them with a strike or a frictional rub.  This approach is well-established: prior work has successfully done real-time singing-bowl synthesis by modelling each partial with tuned digital waveguides and simulating the mallet’s friction.  By indexing modes by size and material (metal vs quartz), and letting mallet hardness shape the excitation, the module can capture both the physics and the musical nuance.  Performance-wise, a dozen 2nd-order resonators is lightweight, so even a high-quality, realistic model (with nonlinearity and multi-mode beating) should easily run at audio rate in Rack.  In short, use multi-mode resonant filters (or banded waveguides) with parameters set by bowl dimensions and material, excited by velocity or friction inputs, to achieve a richly resonant singing bowl sound.
