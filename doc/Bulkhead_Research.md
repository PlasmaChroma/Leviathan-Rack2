# Bulkhead Design Research for VCV Rack

## Bulkhead should become a hybrid room reverb

Your draft already has the right core identity: Bulkhead is not just “another algorithmic reverb,” but a room-modeling reverb with movable boundaries, source and listener placement, material-aware reflections, interpolated delays, and an FDN-style late field exposed through a visual editor. That is a strong foundation, and it is much closer to a credible interactive room module than a generic plate-style effect wrapped in room graphics. fileciteturn0file0

The key architectural decision is where to spend physical accuracy and where to spend statistical density. Modern reverberation work still clusters into three broad families: delay networks, convolution-based methods, and physically based room models. Convolution is accurate for static rooms but is less flexible and can be computationally expensive for long impulse responses or changing conditions; physically based methods are controllable and potentially accurate, but can be too heavy for interactive real-time use; and image-source methods are excellent for specular reflections in simple rectangular rooms, but their cost grows badly with reflection order. That combination of tradeoffs is why a **hybrid** design is the right fit for Bulkhead. citeturn20view0turn15search1turn18view1turn21search13

The most practical inference for a first real Rack release is a **top-down 2D interface backed by a 2.5D shoebox room model**. In other words, the user edits `x/y` positions and wall dimensions directly on the panel, while height, floor/ceiling material, and optional listener/source elevation live as hidden or secondary parameters. That keeps the UI intuitive, aligns with the rectangular-room assumptions used by image-source and SDN work, and still leaves room for draggable speaker icons and listener pose without turning the module into a tiny CAD package. Historical multichannel reverb work also supports this direction: source location sensitivity matters, and adding multiple logical sources need not explode memory or destroy the spatial logic of the design. citeturn20view0turn33view0

## Recommended signal architecture

A real room impulse response has a recognizable structure: direct sound first, then discrete early reflections, then a dense late tail. Classical reverb research frames the early response as roughly the first few tens of milliseconds up to around 50–100 ms, after which the ear increasingly hears a diffuse decay rather than individually meaningful echoes. Early reflections contribute important perceptual cues about room size, source position, incident direction, and spaciousness; they should therefore be computed from geometry, not guessed by static tap patterns. citeturn31view1turn33view0turn32search16

For Bulkhead, the front end should be explicitly geometric. The direct path delay is `fs * distance / c`. Early reflection delays should come from an image-source pass over the shoebox walls, with first-order reflections always active and second-order reflections optional in higher quality modes. Reflection gain can stay physically interpretable and efficient: a useful baseline is distance attenuation proportional to `1 / r`, multiplied by per-surface amplitude reflectances derived from absorption, i.e. `k = sqrt(1 - absorption)`. That formulation is directly consistent with multichannel reverb design literature and with modern SDN material modeling. It also gives Bulkhead a meaningful “drag the speaker, hear the room change” identity instead of a cosmetic one. citeturn33view0turn22view2

The late field should remain a delay-network core, but it should be **driven by the room model** rather than floating as an unrelated generic tail. The best fit is an FDN-based late engine whose delay lengths, damping targets, and stereo decorrelation behavior are derived from the room state. That preserves the classic FDN design advantage: start from a lossless prototype, then introduce attenuation filters to shape frequency-dependent decay. It also keeps room scaling simple, since lossless FDN designs are specifically valuable when delay lengths need to scale with modeled room dimensions and mixing time. citeturn24view1turn20view0

Bulkhead should not ship with only one hidden “quality” topology. The evidence argues for three internal modes. An **Eco** mode can use an optimized small FDN, roughly 8×8; a **Studio** mode can use an optimized 12×12 network; and a **HiFi** mode should use a 16×16 network. That recommendation is grounded in two complementary findings: recent differentiable FDN work shows that small FDNs can be optimized offline to reduce perceptual coloration while saving computation, while a plugin study found that order 16 was the smallest tested FDN size that consistently built echo density fast enough for smooth, artifact-free reverberation across matrix types, and that CPU cost rose steeply beyond that. In practice, that makes 16 a sensible upper quality ceiling for Rack v1, not a default requirement for every patch. citeturn24view2turn24view5turn30view0

For the feedback matrix, Bulkhead should expose **behavior**, not math jargon, to the user, but internally the choice matters. Hadamard matrices are orthogonal, have equal-magnitude elements, and can be implemented with a fast transform costing `N log N`; Householder matrices are also orthogonal but cost only `2N` operations and are attractive when CPU is tight. The practical conclusion is straightforward: use a recursively embedded Householder-style design in lower-cost modes, and allow a Hadamard-centered high-quality option when you want a denser, more uniformly mixed tail. Even better, pre-optimize the small-network matrices offline and ship them as fixed constants rather than “discovering” them at runtime. citeturn25view1turn25view2turn30view0

## Real-time modulation without clicks or zipper noise

This is where Bulkhead can win or lose. Time-varying delay lines are unavoidable when walls move, speakers move, or the listener moves. The fractional-delay literature is clear that these filters are foundational for audio applications, and it also makes an important distinction: FIR-style interpolation is naturally friendlier in time-varying situations, while recursive allpass fractional delays can introduce transients when coefficients change unless their internal state is handled carefully. The literature also notes that Thiran-style allpass designs are attractive for narrow-band approximation, whereas Lagrange-style polynomial interpolation is well suited to frequently changing delay values. Hermite-based designs are attractive because they preserve a good passband while improving sidelobe behavior at moderate complexity. Taken together, the sound-quality-biased choice for Bulkhead is to spend better interpolation on the **direct and early paths**, where the ear localizes changes most easily. citeturn27search6turn27search7turn34search0turn28search0

A strong implementation choice is therefore this split: use **4-point Lagrange or cubic Hermite interpolation** on the direct path and geometric early reflections, and reserve cheaper first-order allpass interpolation for internal late-network plumbing only when it is effectively inaudible. That aligns with your draft’s instinct to use cubic Hermite interpolation, but tightens where it matters most. With that choice, “sound quality costs CPU” in the places listeners actually notice, instead of burning cycles deep inside a diffuse tail where the return is smaller. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn28search0turn34search0

Bulkhead’s **movement** parameter should primarily rely on **slow modulation of the late-network feedback matrix**, not only on brute-force modulation of many long delay lines. Schlecht and Habets showed that nearly continuous feedback-matrix modulation can make the tail livelier while producing less likely perceivable artifacts, and that unitary matrix modulation can be guaranteed stable. That is a better late-tail motion strategy than aggressively wobbling every delay line in the network. If you do modulate embedded allpass structures, Werner’s energy-preserving time-varying Schroeder allpass work is the right reference point, because classic Schroeder allpasses do not preserve energy under arbitrary coefficient variation. citeturn38view0turn35view0

Parameter smoothing should be a first-class system, not an afterthought. A modern SDN implementation smoothed position and rotation parameters over **20 ms** specifically to avoid artifacts, and identified filter redesign as the heaviest runtime step. That gives Bulkhead a good default policy: smooth positional and orientation parameters over roughly 10–20 ms; treat large discontinuities such as preset recalls or hard CV jumps with a short crossfade between old and new geometric states; and never redesign material filters sample-by-sample. Precompute as much as possible, especially distance-dependent air absorption and material-filter families. citeturn23view3turn22view3

Delay-line selection inside the late engine also matters more than it first appears. The FDN plugin study is blunt on this point: too-short delays, too-narrow delay ranges, or poorly distributed lengths create clustering, segmentation, and slow echo-density growth; low orders such as 2, 4, and often 8 are not smooth enough; and order 16 was the smallest tested size that consistently reached useful smoothness. CPU cost also rose sharply with order in that study, from 13.3% at order 16 with fixed coefficients to 30.4% at 32 and 92.5% at 64. For Bulkhead, that means: use balanced, incommensurate delay lengths; do not default above 16 in Rack v1; and let small-FDN optimization do the heavy lifting in the lower modes. citeturn30view0turn24view2

## Materials, room semantics, and spatial control

Wall materials should be modeled as **octave-band absorption presets** under the hood, even if the panel presents them more musically. That is the standard practical description in room-acoustic plugin work. The useful chain is: store or interpolate octave-band absorption coefficients `α(ω)`, convert them to reflectance magnitude with `|R(jω)| = sqrt(1 - α(ω))`, densify the sparse band points by interpolation, and fit a **minimum-phase low-order IIR** that can run per wall or per reflection path. A recent SDN plugin used third-order IIR filters and showed that this was accurate enough for real-time use, while keeping runtime cost controlled. That makes a strong default design for Bulkhead’s materials. citeturn22view3turn22view2

The panel should not expose “octave-band absorption” directly unless the user asks for it. The main UI layer should expose **presets plus two or three musically meaningful macros**: something like *Material*, *Absorb*, and *Diffuse*. Internally, *Material* chooses a preset family such as plaster, brick, wood, glass, curtain, carpet, foam, or metal; *Absorb* scales that preset toward more or less energy loss; and *Diffuse* can bias the late tail toward a more decorrelated density build-up or a more specular room character. The underlying math remains grounded, but the user interaction becomes immediate. This is an inference from the material-filter literature and from your draft’s emphasis on intuitive manipulation rather than parameter spreadsheets. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn22view3

Your instinct to add draggable speaker icons is especially strong. Earlier multichannel reverberator work explicitly emphasized that the reverberant response should be spatially sensitive to source location, that the directional growth of reverberation contributes to the sense of source position and room extent, and that several unique input sources can be incorporated without a prohibitive memory penalty. Bulkhead should therefore present **source icons as speakers** when the input is stereo: left and right sources are linked by default, preserving their spacing when dragged, with an option to unlink them for asymmetrical rooms. In mono mode, a single source icon is cleaner. citeturn33view0

The listener should be a head icon with **position plus yaw** on the main panel. Full yaw/pitch/roll support is possible and has precedent in recent SDN work, but only yaw deserves precious front-panel real estate in a Rack module; pitch, roll, and height can live in a context menu, a side panel, or a future “advanced view.” That gives the user the perceptually important control immediately while keeping the top-down panel readable. citeturn23view3

An **air absorption** toggle is worth including, but it should be optional and probably off by default in Eco mode. The 2024 SDN implementation treated air absorption as a separate filter stage and found that enabling it had only a small CPU impact. That is attractive because it adds a believable “distance dries the highs” effect without forcing every patch into a maximum-physics mode. It also gives Bulkhead one more layer of room realism that users can hear, not just admire in a tooltip. citeturn20view0turn22view3

## Rack-native interface design

The visual room editor should be implemented as a **custom Widget inside a FramebufferWidget**. Rack’s own documentation is explicit that `Widget::draw()` is called every screen frame, and that complex custom widgets with many paths or lots of text can consume meaningful CPU/GPU resources. The official remedy is framebuffer caching and explicit dirtying only when something actually changes. Bulkhead’s room canvas is almost the textbook case for this pattern: the room outline, wall fills, material strips, and idle icons are mostly static until the user drags something or a mapped parameter changes. citeturn11view0turn39view1

Interaction-wise, Rack already gives you the event hooks you need. `ButtonEvent` must be consumed for drag events to fire, and the base widget API exposes `onDragStart`, `onDragMove`, `onDragEnd`, `onDragHover`, `onDoubleClick`, and context-menu hooks. That is enough to support a polished room canvas with wall handles, draggable source/speaker icons, a draggable listener head, double-click reset behavior, and right-click menus for precise values or preset selection. Standard knobs remain useful for macro controls, but the room canvas should not be reduced to a decorative display sitting above twelve unrelated knobs. citeturn39view0turn39view1turn39view2

The front panel should be dominated by the room canvas. Below or beside it, the macro layer should stay compact: *Size*, *Decay*, *Motion*, *Diffuse*, *Early/Late*, *Material*, *Mix*, and perhaps *Air* or *Brightness*. The canvas itself should show wall colors or textures representing materials; ghosted first-order ray paths when a “show reflections” overlay is enabled; and a highlighted “active target” ring when CV is mapped to source or listener movement. Rack’s `drawLayer()` support is useful here, because the active speaker and listener markers can remain visible on the self-illuminating layer even against darker room themes. citeturn11view0turn39view1

One more Rack-specific detail matters: do not let the UI become the hidden performance villain. Rack’s manual notes that frame rate affects GPU consumption almost proportionally, and the plugin guide warns against holding `Font` objects across frames because they are tied to Rack’s window object lifecycle. Bulkhead’s UI should therefore redraw only on actual state changes, keep heavy labels minimal, and avoid any analysis rendering that depends on the audio thread. The room should feel alive, but the panel cannot become a tiny oscilloscope app hiding inside a reverb. citeturn11view2turn11view0

## Implementation roadmap and validation

The cleanest build sequence is staged. First, implement the geometric front end for direct sound and first-order reflections with stable interpolation and a simple stereo renderer. Next, add second-order reflections and wall-material filtering. Then add the quality-scaled FDN late core and the early/late crossover logic. Only after that should you wire in motion modulation, air absorption, and the full room canvas. This order mirrors the structure of room-response perception and makes debugging vastly easier, because each stage can be impulse-tested and listened to in isolation. That same staged logic is visible in both historical early-response designs and modern interactive room plugins. citeturn33view0turn20view0

Performance targets need to be defined in **Rack terms**, not only in generic DSP terms. Rack’s engine CPU use scales almost proportionally with sample rate, so Bulkhead should be profiled at least at 48, 96, and 192 kHz. Rack also supports up to 16 channels per polyphonic cable; a naïve “independent full room simulation per channel” design would therefore scale disastrously. The right v1 product decision is to make Bulkhead explicitly **mono/stereo first**, with any higher-channel ambitions deferred to later versions or a separate variant. That choice is much better than pretending to support full polyphony while silently producing unusable CPU spikes. citeturn11view2turn11view0

Validation should combine physical, numerical, and perceptual checks. Physically, direct-path and reflection times should match geometry-derived expectations, and aggregate decay should roughly track Sabine/Eyring-style expectations as room size and absorption change. Numerically, impulse responses should be checked for click-free movement, coefficient-jump resilience, denormal safety, and stable decay. Perceptually, the critical listening tests are metallic ringing, flutter, early-response raggedness on impulses, excessive mush when reflections are too dense, and the subjective liveliness of the modulated late tail. Those are not arbitrary concerns; they map directly onto long-standing reverb quality criteria such as flatness, high echo density, avoidance of flutter, and realistic transient behavior. citeturn18view0turn20view0turn31view1turn33view0

The most defensible concrete specification for **Bulkhead v1** is this: a top-down shoebox room editor; one listener with position and yaw; one mono source or a linked stereo speaker pair; exact direct sound plus first-order reflections, with second-order reflections in higher quality modes; octave-band wall presets rendered through fitted minimum-phase material filters; an optimized 8×8 Eco, 12×12 Studio, and 16×16 HiFi late engine; slow, stable motion via feedback-matrix modulation; optional air absorption; and a cached custom Rack widget as the primary interface.
